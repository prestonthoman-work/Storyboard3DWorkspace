# Storyboard 3D

A visionOS app for planning a film shot by shot. You set up a small 3D set on a real table, arrange objects, save keyframes, and photograph each shot from where you're standing. The result is a storyboard you can play full screen or export as a video or a printable sheet.

## How it works

1. **Set the stage.** The round base finds a table in your room and sits on it. Either way it sits beside the control panel (to its left, since the timeline opens on the right) rather than behind it; if no table is found, it floats there. Choose a surface, resize the base, spin it by its edge, or slide it by the brass handle on its edge (along the table, or anywhere when it's floating), and pick a backdrop (a built-in sky or your own image).
2. **Build the set.** Place objects from the library, describe one in a prompt (generated by Claude), or import your own 3D model (a USDZ file, such as one from Reality Composer Pro or Object Capture). An imported model is sized to match the built-in objects, centered, and set on the base; it's copied into the project, so it travels with duplicates and reopens with the project. Pinch and drag to arrange them; they fall under gravity and stack on each other. Grabbing only moves an object (and a second hand resizes it): it keeps its angle however your wrist turns. To change its angle, use the selected object's rings: the flat cyan ring at its foot turns it, and the upright brass arc over it tilts it forward or backward about its own side-to-side axis. Both turn it in place around its center, show the angle while you drag, and work the same after the base has been spun. The corner handle resizes it. Double-tap an object to cycle its color (after the last color it returns to its original look), or pick a color under its row in the Objects tab or from the Color button above it. Taps are read from the pinches themselves, so a grab, or letting go and quickly grabbing again, never counts as a double-tap.
   **Snapping** is gentle and comes in two separate kinds, each with its own toggle under the panel. Position snapping lines an object's center up with another's when you let go within a few millimeters of a guide: while you move it, a faint line and outline show where it would land, and moving on past it is all it takes to place it freely. It never pulls an object while it's in your hand and never changes its rotation, and an object you pick up already lined up can be nudged without being pulled back. Rotation snapping makes the rings click onto 15° steps when close to one, and moves freely between them. Both are in the Snapping menu under the panel; hold Option on a connected keyboard to skip snapping for a moment.
   **Characters.** Three built-in poseable figures (Mannequin, Child, Stocky Figure) share one 17-joint rig: hips, waist, chest, neck and head, plus a shoulder, elbow and hand on each arm and a hip, knee and foot on each leg. In the scene a character moves, turns and resizes like any object; you can recolor it and adjust its height, build, head and legs. Posing happens in the **Pose Studio**, a separate volume showing the character about 55 cm tall on a small walnut-and-brass stage. Lower it by its window bar onto a table and it settles there like a miniature on the tabletop (the system snaps it to the surface; with permission to sense your surroundings, the studio's panel confirms it's on a table). Nothing moves it while you pose; drag the bar to move it again, or leave it floating wherever is comfortable. In the studio, pinch any body part and drag it to swing it at its joint, or grab a hand or foot and the arm or leg bends to follow (inverse kinematics). The panel beside it holds every control: undo and redo, every body part with exact angle sliders and ±5° steps (and for hands and feet, the elbow or knee direction and position), an orange Reset for the selected part, and a red Reset Whole Pose. Every joint has limits, and body parts can't pass through each other: an arm or leg dragged or slid into the body stops at its surface and slides along it (a little overlap is allowed, as with the real wooden figure). The Poses menu has ten presets (sitting says it needs a prop to sit on), Mirror Pose, and your own saved poses, which work in any project. Drag the studio floor, or use the front/side/back buttons, to look from any side. Edits go straight to the character in the scene, with undo.
   **Characters in the scene.** **Look At** keeps a character's head, neck and chest turned toward another object or character (at its eyes), as far as its joints allow, following it as either moves, through playback and renders, until Stop Looking or you pose the head by hand. **Sit On** puts a character on an object: its legs bend to the seat's height, its feet stay on the ground, and it moves so its hips are on top. **Lean On** rests the character's back or a shoulder against an object at least shoulder high (it turns that side to the object, tilts toward it from the feet, and slides until it touches), or puts a hand on an object: on top of anything below the shoulder, such as a counter, or on the side of anything taller. It can also half-sit on an edge at hip height, or lean in on its elbows at a table. If the object is out of reach, the character steps closer. Sitting and leaning find the object's actual surface, so round rocks and slanted roofs work. **Hold Hands** joins the nearest hands of two characters. Each hand can be relaxed, open, a fist, pointing or gripping, and a hand closes round anything it holds. With **Walk Between Keyframes** on, a character that moves from one keyframe's spot to the next walks there, facing the way it goes. Any object can be **put in a character's hand**; each keyframe records what's in which hand, so an object can be picked up or put down partway through: held at both ends of a stretch it follows the hand, held at one end it moves between the hand and where it rests. Pinching and dragging a held object adjusts the grip. The **Eyelines** toggle shows a cone from each character's eyes, plus the 180° line when the scene has two characters; like labels, the guides never appear in photos.
3. **Animate.** The **storyboard window** opens with the Animate tab: every shot as a panel, four to a row, grouped by scene and labeled the way storyboard artists do (1A, 1B, 1C, then 2A when a new scene starts). Name a scene by typing in its heading; start a new scene with New Scene, or from a panel's menu (Start New Scene Here, Join Scene, Duplicate, Move, Delete); drag panels to reorder. Above the panels are playback and a slim timeline for timing, and Present plays the storyboard full screen. Each shot is a keyframe: it saves where everything is, including each character's pose. Move things and add another keyframe, and playback glides between them. Choose **Hold, then Cut** on a keyframe to keep its poses until the next one, for storyboards of distinct poses. Each keyframe can hold a shot type (wide, close-up, and so on) and notes, which you can type or dictate.
4. **Shoot.** Look at the scene through the viewfinder and press the shutter (the round button under the panel, on any tab) to take a photo for the selected shot. The **Library** (in the storyboard window, or Photo Library on the Camera tab) shows every photo in the project, labeled with its shot or Not Used; pick one to see it large, use it for any shot, save, share, or delete it. The camera settings set any focal length from 12 to 200 mm (with 18–135 mm presets), the frame shape (16:9, 1.85, 2.39, 4:3, 1:1 or vertical 9:16; narrower shapes get side bars so every export stays 16:9), and optional depth of field: pick an f-stop and focus on the center of the frame or a chosen object. Lighting and film look are adjustable too, and photos re-render automatically when their keyframe changes.
   **Backdrop Image** (on the Base tab, under the backdrop picker) sets whether the backdrop stays in the room: **Always Show**, or **Hide Between Photos**, which keeps it out of the way while you build and brings it up when you press the shutter (or Show to Frame). The backdrop keeps to the base itself, so moving an object far out doesn't push it away. It stays up through the countdown and developing, and hides again once the photo is taken or you leave the camera; if a photo fails, it stays up so you can try again. The choice is remembered and applied from the moment the app opens, and showing the image for a photo never changes it. Photos, re-shoots and exports always include the backdrop either way.
   Depth of field is made the way a real lens makes it: each photo is rendered 16–48 times from points across the lens opening and averaged, so a photo with it takes several times longer. Where the renders disagree a lot (only out of focus), the result is softened so a strong blur stays smooth. It shows in photos, slideshows and a quick **Preview Shot** in the camera settings, not in the live viewfinder; the animatic can include it at lower quality (slower to export).
5. **Share.** Play the storyboard full screen, or export:
   - an **animatic** (MP4): objects move while the camera glides between your photo angles
   - a **slideshow** (MP4) of each shot's photo, with optional captions ("SHOT 1C · WS · …")
   - a **storyboard sheet** (PDF), with 2, 6 or 12 shots per page
   - a **shot list** (CSV) with each shot's scene and label, type, notes, lens, aperture, frame, lighting, and who's in it

   Slideshow captions and the sheet show each shot's camera settings (such as "35 mm · f/2 · 2.39:1"). With **Crop to Frame Shape** on, videos and the sheet take the camera's frame shape instead of 16:9 with bars, such as a real vertical 810 × 1440 video.

Projects save automatically. If a save fails, the workspace shows a banner with a Try Again button, and the app won't switch projects until the changes are saved.

> **Characters and imported models.** Only the built-in characters are poseable. Imported, generated and sample objects move, turn and resize as a whole; nothing is rigged automatically, and imported models keep their own colors (they can't be recolored). Only USDZ files can be imported, since other USD files can point at textures stored beside them. If a project's model file goes missing, a gray box stands in for it and the project says so.

> **Generating objects from prompts.** With a Claude API key added (Objects panel → Connect Claude), a typed prompt such as "a park bench" is designed by Claude (`claude-opus-5-5`, through the Anthropic Messages API): it describes the object as simple shapes (boxes, rounded boxes, spheres, cylinders, cones and roof-like wedges, each optionally turned) at real-world size, and the app builds them in the same low-poly style as the built-in objects, shrunk to the characters' scale so a chair is the right height to sit on. Each object is one request billed to your Anthropic account and usually takes several seconds to a minute. A generated object is saved as its list of shapes, so it reopens exactly the same without calling Claude again; Generate again for a different take. It can be recolored (its main body takes the new color), but like other objects it isn't rigged. Prompts naming a person still give a poseable character, prompts that only name a built-in object ("a red house") use it, and base prompts still pick a built-in template. Connecting checks the key with Claude before saving it. Without a key, the prompt is matched against the built-in catalog instead (anything unknown becomes a placeholder). With a key, a request that fails places nothing: the reason (such as a rejected key, no credit, or a timeout) shows in red under the prompt, with Try Again. The key is stored in the device's Keychain; when running from Xcode you can instead set `ANTHROPIC_API_KEY` in the scheme's environment. To use a different service, see `ModelGenerationServiceProvider` in `Services/ModelGenerationService.swift`.

## Requirements

- Xcode with the visionOS 27.2 SDK
- Apple Vision Pro, or the visionOS Simulator. The Simulator has no table detection, so the base floats in front of you there.
- An Anthropic API key and an internet connection, to generate objects from prompts. Without them, prompts fall back to the built-in catalog.

The app asks for these permissions:

| Permission | Used for |
| --- | --- |
| World sensing | Finding tables to put the base on, and telling a table from other surfaces when the Pose Studio is set down |
| Microphone and speech recognition | Dictating shot notes |
| Add to Photos | Saving a captured shot to Photos |

## Build and run

Open `Storyboard3DWorkspace.xcodeproj` in Xcode, select the **Storyboard3DWorkspace** scheme and a Vision Pro destination, then press Run.

From the command line:

```sh
xcodebuild build -scheme Storyboard3DWorkspace \
  -destination 'platform=visionOS Simulator,name=Apple Vision Pro'
```

## Tests

The tests use Swift Testing and run on the Simulator:

```sh
xcodebuild test -scheme Storyboard3DWorkspace \
  -destination 'platform=visionOS Simulator,name=Apple Vision Pro'
```

Tests that save projects point `ProjectStore.storageOverride` at a temporary folder, so your real projects are never read or changed.

## Code layout

| Folder | What's in it |
| --- | --- |
| `AppModel.swift` | App-wide state: the open project, objects, keyframes, capture, undo, and saving. Views read and change the app through it. |
| `Models/` | Value types and small controllers: keyframe timeline and animator, photo library, camera paths, the capture camera (`CaptureController`), keeping keyframe photos up to date (`PhotoSyncController`), export (`ExportController`), character posing and saved poses (`PoseEditor`). |
| `Scene/` | RealityKit content: the workspace scene, base templates, sample objects, gravity, selection handles, table anchoring (`TableAnchoring`). |
| `Services/` | Rendering, export writers, project storage (`ProjectStore`), imported models (`ImportedModels`), spatial tracking, dictation, and model generation. |
| `Views/` | SwiftUI panels and windows: the workspace tabs, timeline window, storyboard viewer, projects list. |

Projects are stored in Application Support under `Projects/<id>/`. The comment at the top of `Services/ProjectStore.swift` describes the files in each project folder.
